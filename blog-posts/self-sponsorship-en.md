<p>One of the most frequently asked questions we receive at Imin2UK is about self-sponsorship. Before we get into the details, there is one critical point you need to understand from the outset: <strong>there is no such thing as a "self-sponsorship visa" in UK immigration law.</strong> It is not a visa category. It is not an official immigration route. It simply does not exist as a formal visa type.</p>

<p>If you have heard of someone who "came to the UK on a self-sponsorship visa," they most likely do not fully understand which visa they actually hold. And if an immigration agency is advertising a "self-sponsorship visa service," they are almost certainly using the term as a marketing label for something else entirely. What people refer to as self-sponsorship is not a named visa — it is a methodology that works around the existing Skilled Worker sponsorship system.</p>

<h2>Why Is Self-Sponsorship Being Talked About?</h2>

<p>As UK immigration policy has tightened significantly in recent years, the range of occupations eligible for the Skilled Worker visa has been dramatically reduced. Previously, medium-skilled roles — such as restaurant chefs — were relatively accessible routes into UK employment-based immigration. That is no longer the case. The system now restricts sponsorship almost exclusively to high-skilled occupations.</p>

<p>This shift has left many people who would previously have qualified for a Skilled Worker visa searching for alternative ways into the UK. Self-sponsorship is the concept that emerges in response to this frustration — and it is worth understanding properly, because there are scenarios where it makes sense and many where it does not.</p>

<h2>How Self-Sponsorship Actually Works</h2>

<p>The underlying principle is straightforward. A person based in Korea (or any other country) incorporates a company in the UK. Under UK company law, there are no restrictions preventing a foreign national from setting up a UK limited company. You can own 100% of the shares, become the Managing Director, and structure the company however you like — all without needing to be in the UK or hold any UK immigration status.</p>

<p>Once the company is established, it applies to the UK Home Office for a <strong>Skilled Worker Sponsorship Licence</strong> — the same licence that any UK employer would need in order to hire a non-UK/non-Irish national. If the licence is granted, the company can issue a Certificate of Sponsorship (CoS) to a prospective employee. In this case, the "employee" would be the company's own founder and owner.</p>

<p>That person then uses the CoS to apply for a Skilled Worker visa and enters the UK as an employee of their own company.</p>

<p>It sounds circular, but it is theoretically legal. Under the old Tier 2 (General) visa system, immigration rules explicitly prohibited visa holders from owning more than 10% of the sponsoring company. That restriction was removed when the visa was renamed the Skilled Worker visa. There is therefore no longer a rule that explicitly bars someone from being both the owner and the sponsored employee of the same company.</p>

<p>Setting up a UK limited company itself is also remarkably simple. There are no minimum capital requirements and very few restrictions on business type. You can even purchase a pre-formed "shelf company" — a ready-made company with standard articles of association and basic configurations already in place — for as little as £50 to a few hundred pounds. The company formation is not the hard part.</p>

<h2>Where It Gets Complicated: Sponsorship Licence Requirements</h2>

<p>The moment a company applies for a Skilled Worker Sponsorship Licence, UK immigration law takes over — and this is where self-sponsorship arrangements run into serious difficulty.</p>

<p>Here are the key requirements for obtaining a sponsorship licence, and why each one creates a problem for someone attempting self-sponsorship from overseas:</p>

<h3>1. Authorising Officer and Level 1 User</h3>

<p>Every sponsor must designate an <strong>Authorising Officer (AO)</strong> who takes legal responsibility for the licence, and at least one <strong>Level 1 User</strong> to manage it on a day-to-day basis. The Level 1 User must be a British citizen or settled person. They cannot simply be a nominee or a paper representative — they must be a genuinely engaged member of the organisation.</p>

<p>If you are currently based in Korea and have not yet entered the UK, you will have no UK address and no National Insurance number. You will inevitably need to bring in someone to fill this role — either by hiring a senior employee at a full market salary, or by using a stand-in arrangement that the Home Office is likely to scrutinise very carefully.</p>

<h3>2. Organisational Chart</h3>

<p>Companies with fewer than 50 employees must submit a full organisational chart. For a newly established company with no actual staff, producing a credible organisational chart is either very difficult or impossible.</p>

<h3>3. UK Bank Statements</h3>

<p>If the company has been operating for fewer than 18 months, it must provide UK business bank account statements. Again, a company set up purely in advance of a visa application will have no genuine trading history to show.</p>

<h3>4. Genuine Vacancy Test</h3>

<p>This is perhaps the most important hurdle. The Home Office will assess whether the role being sponsored is a <strong>genuine vacancy</strong> — meaning the company has a real, operational need to fill that position. They will look at why the role exists, what business activity it supports, and whether the company is actually trading.</p>

<p>A newly incorporated company with no revenue, no premises, and no employees — applying to sponsor its own founder at a high salary — is going to face very difficult questions. If the Home Office concludes that the primary purpose of the arrangement is to obtain immigration status rather than to fill a genuine business need, the licence application will be refused.</p>

<h2>When Self-Sponsorship Can Work</h2>

<p>With all of that said, self-sponsorship is not inherently impossible. There are circumstances in which it can be a legitimate and viable route.</p>

<p>If you have substantial capital, a credible business model, and the resources to build a genuine UK operation from the outset — including leasing real premises, hiring a Level 1 User at a proper market salary, and staffing key functions such as HR, finance, product, and sales — then the conditions for a sponsorship licence become far more achievable. A well-resourced, properly structured UK business with real commercial activity is in a very different position from a shell company set up purely for visa purposes.</p>

<p>There is no fixed figure for how much capital is required, because it varies enormously depending on the industry, location, and scale of the business. But the honest answer is that the kind of investment needed to make self-sponsorship work properly is significant.</p>

<p>It is also worth noting that if you are already running a business in Korea, there are more conventional immigration routes designed specifically for your situation. The <strong><a href="/gbm-uk-expansion-worker-en">Global Business Mobility (GBM) UK Expansion Worker</a></strong> route, for example, is designed for companies expanding into the UK from an established overseas base. If that route is not suitable for your circumstances, self-sponsorship may be worth considering as an alternative — but only alongside genuine business intent and sufficient resources.</p>

<h2>When Self-Sponsorship Does Not Work</h2>

<p>Self-sponsorship is not a workaround for the fact that your occupation no longer qualifies for a Skilled Worker visa. It is not a way to "buy" a visa. Approaching it with that mindset is likely to result in significant financial loss and, potentially, visa refusal or cancellation.</p>

<p>Even if a visa is initially granted, the Home Office conducts ongoing audits of sponsor companies. They review tax records and monitor whether companies are genuinely trading in line with their stated business plans. If a company is found to exist only on paper, or if its real activities do not match what was represented in the licence application, the sponsorship licence can be revoked. And if the licence is revoked, your visa will be curtailed — meaning you and any family members in the UK on dependent visas will be required to leave.</p>

<h2>Practical Advice Based on Your Situation</h2>

<h3>If You Are Currently Based in Korea</h3>

<p>If you have a business idea you want to bring to the UK, our strong recommendation is to <strong>test it in Korea first</strong>. Develop your business model, validate your revenue model, and build a track record. Then consider entering the UK market through a branch or subsidiary — the GBM UK Expansion Worker route is designed precisely for this kind of phased market entry.</p>

<p>If you want to be in the UK as quickly as possible, and your age allows it, consider entering on a <strong><a href="/youth-mobility-scheme-en">Youth Mobility Scheme (YMS) visa</a></strong>, a student visa leading to a <strong><a href="/graduate-visa-en">Graduate visa</a></strong>, or an <strong><a href="/high-potential-individual-en">HPI visa</a></strong> if you qualify. These routes allow you to live and work (or run a business) in the UK freely, without sponsorship. Use that time to test your business idea, build a track record, and then apply for a sponsorship licence from a position of genuine commercial activity.</p>

<h3>If You Are Already in the UK</h3>

<p>Do not wait until your current visa is about to expire and then attempt self-sponsorship as a last resort. Instead, use the time you have to genuinely develop your business idea. Test it, validate it, and build a real operating history. When you have demonstrable commercial traction, applying for a sponsorship licence from that position of genuine activity gives you a far stronger case.</p>

<h2>The Honest Summary</h2>

<p>Self-sponsorship, properly understood, is one of several possible routes for those with a genuine, well-resourced plan to build a business in the UK. For the right person — with serious capital, a credible business model, and a realistic long-term plan — it is worth exploring alongside other options.</p>

<p>But for those who simply find that their occupation no longer qualifies for a Skilled Worker visa and are looking for an easy or inexpensive way to obtain immigration status, self-sponsorship is not the answer. The Home Office is very effective at identifying arrangements that exist primarily to obtain visas rather than to run genuine businesses. The financial and immigration consequences of getting this wrong are serious.</p>

<p>Before proceeding with any business-based immigration strategy, ask yourself honestly: Is my business plan credible enough to withstand Home Office scrutiny? Do I have sufficient capital to sustain a real UK operation for five to ten years?</p>
