<p>If you have been living in the UK for a long time, you may be considering applying for Indefinite Leave to Remain (ILR) through the Long Residence route — commonly referred to as the 10-year ILR. While the concept sounds straightforward, there are far more conditions to check than simply counting ten years of residence. This guide covers everything you need to know, from basic eligibility to the specific requirements and common pitfalls that can catch applicants off guard.</p>

<h2>Basic Eligibility: Three Core Conditions</h2>

<p>Before diving into the details, there are three fundamental conditions every applicant must meet:</p>

<ul>
  <li>You must have lived lawfully in the UK for a continuous period of <strong>10 years</strong></li>
  <li>You must be physically present in the UK <strong>on the date of your application</strong></li>
  <li>Dependants <strong>cannot</strong> be included in this application — a spouse or children must apply through their own separate routes</li>
</ul>

<h2>Which Visa Types Count Towards the 10 Years?</h2>

<p>The 10-year period is calculated based on time spent in the UK under a <strong>valid visa or leave</strong>. However, certain types of permission do <strong>not</strong> count toward the qualifying period:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Visitor visa</strong> stays</li>
  <li><strong>Short-term student (English language)</strong> visa stays</li>
  <li><strong>Seasonal Worker</strong> visa stays</li>
</ul>

<p>If any portion of your time in the UK was spent on one of these visa types, that period will not be included when calculating your 10 years.</p>

<h2>Absence from the UK: The Rules You Must Know</h2>

<p>Continuous residence does not mean you can never leave the UK — but your absences must remain within strict limits. This area has become more complex following a rule change introduced on <strong>11 April 2024</strong>.</p>

<h3>The Universal Rule (Applies to All Periods)</h3>

<p>Across your entire 10-year period, you must not have spent more than <strong>180 days outside the UK in any rolling 12-month period</strong>. Importantly, "any 12-month period" does not mean a fixed calendar year — it means that no matter which date you choose as a starting point for a 12-month window, the total number of days spent abroad must not exceed 180.</p>

<p>For example, if you spent 100 days in Korea towards the end of one year and then immediately spent another 100 days in Korea at the start of the following year, each calendar year might appear to be within the 180-day limit. However, measured against a rolling 12-month window straddling both years, you would have been abroad for 200 days — which breaks the continuous residence requirement.</p>

<h3>Rules for Periods Before 11 April 2024</h3>

<p>For any part of your 10-year period that falls <strong>before 11 April 2024</strong>, the following additional conditions applied:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Total absences during that period must not exceed <strong>548 days</strong></li>
  <li>No single continuous absence must exceed <strong>184 days</strong></li>
</ul>

<h3>Rules for Periods From 11 April 2024 Onwards</h3>

<p>For periods <strong>from 11 April 2024 onwards</strong>, the 548-day total and 184-day single-trip limits have been removed. Only the universal rolling 180-day rule applies.</p>

<p>If you are currently preparing to apply for the 10-year ILR — or plan to do so in the coming years — you will need to carefully calculate your absences by splitting the 10-year window at <strong>11 April 2024</strong> and applying the relevant rules to each portion.</p>

<h3>Exceptions to the Absence Rules</h3>

<p>Certain circumstances may be treated as exceptions and excluded from the absence count:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Unavoidable travel delays due to humanitarian crises, natural disasters, or pandemics</li>
  <li>A life-threatening illness affecting yourself or an immediate family member</li>
  <li>The death of an immediate family member</li>
  <li>Certain research activities carried out on a Skilled Worker visa with sponsor approval</li>
</ul>

<h2>Overstaying and the COVID-19 Exception</h2>

<p>If you have at any point overstayed your visa, it is important to understand that overstay exemption provisions may protect your application from being refused outright — but the overstay period itself does <strong>not</strong> count toward your 10 years. Simply put, you may avoid refusal, but the overstay period will not be added to your qualifying residence.</p>

<p>There is one notable exception: if you were in the UK without valid leave during <strong>August 2020</strong> (specifically between 1 and 31 August 2020), but you held valid leave immediately before that period, your stay during that month may be treated as lawful residence and counted toward the 10 years.</p>

<h2>When Continuous Residence Is Broken</h2>

<p>Certain events will permanently break your continuous residence, resetting the clock to zero:</p>

<ul>
  <li>A custodial sentence (imprisonment)</li>
  <li>A deportation order or exclusion order</li>
  <li>Exceeding the permitted absence limits described above</li>
</ul>

<h3>The Critical Risk: Gaps Between Visas</h3>

<p>One of the most common and costly mistakes in Long Residence ILR applications involves <strong>gaps between visa grants</strong>. The safest approach is to extend your current visa — or switch to a qualifying visa category — before your existing leave expires, while remaining in the UK throughout.</p>

<p>If you are unable to extend or switch from within the UK and must return to your home country, you should apply for your next visa as quickly as possible and return to the UK promptly. If the gap between your departure and your re-entry grows long enough to exceed the rolling 180-day absence limit, your continuous residence will be broken and the entire 10-year clock resets from the moment you re-enter the UK.</p>

<p>To illustrate with a real-world scenario: suppose your Youth Mobility Scheme (YMS) visa expired in February and you returned to Korea, with plans to come back in August on a student visa for a Master's degree. A student visa is typically issued with a start date one month before the course begins, meaning you could potentially re-enter in August. Depending on the exact dates of your departure and re-entry, the gap may or may not exceed 180 days — it could be very close either way. Understanding this risk in advance and planning accordingly is essential.</p>

<h2>Additional Requirements for the ILR Application</h2>

<p>Completing 10 years of continuous qualifying residence is necessary, but not sufficient on its own. You must also satisfy several additional requirements.</p>

<h3>Application Fee</h3>

<p>As of <strong>8 April 2026</strong>, the ILR application fee is <strong>£3,226</strong> (increased from the previous £3,029). The Immigration Health Surcharge (IHS) does not apply to ILR applications.</p>

<h3>Minimum 12 Months on Current Leave</h3>

<p>At the time of your ILR application, you must have been residing in the UK under your current visa for at least <strong>12 months</strong>. Even if your current visa is a qualifying type for the 10-year route, you cannot apply for ILR if you have held that visa for less than one year.</p>

<h3>English Language Requirement</h3>

<p>The required English language level depends on when you submit your application:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Applications submitted <strong>before 26 March 2027</strong>: Speaking and Listening at CEFR level <strong>B1</strong> or above</li>
  <li>Applications submitted <strong>from 26 March 2027 onwards</strong>: Speaking and Listening at CEFR level <strong>B2</strong> or above</li>
</ul>

<p>The English language test requirement can be met alternatively by holding a UK degree or an overseas degree confirmed by Ecctis. Applicants aged <strong>65 or over</strong> are exempt. Those with a long-term physical or mental condition may also apply for an exemption, but must provide a letter from their GP and original medical records to support the request.</p>

<h3>Life in the UK Test</h3>

<p>You must pass the <strong>Life in the UK Test</strong> before applying. Key details:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Test fee: <strong>£50</strong></li>
  <li>Format: 24 questions to be answered within 45 minutes</li>
  <li>Pass mark: <strong>75%</strong> or higher</li>
  <li>Booking: Must be made online at least <strong>3 days before</strong> the test date</li>
  <li>Cancellations and refunds are only possible if cancelled at least <strong>72 hours before</strong> the test</li>
</ul>

<h2>What If You Do Not Meet All the Conditions?</h2>

<p>If you satisfy the 10-year continuous residence requirement but are missing either the English language evidence or the Life in the UK Test result, you may be eligible to apply for a <strong>leave extension of up to 2 years</strong>. Note that this is not an ILR application, so the Immigration Health Surcharge (IHS) will still apply. Once this extended leave is granted, you are free to work, but you will not have access to Public Funds.</p>

<p>If the 10-year continuous residence condition itself is not met, there may be alternative immigration routes to consider — such as <strong>Appendix Private Life</strong> or <strong>Appendix FM</strong>. These routes involve more complex individual assessments, so a dedicated consultation would be needed to determine whether they apply to your circumstances.</p>

<hr>

<p>To summarise: the 10-year Long Residence ILR is not simply a matter of counting ten years. You need to carefully verify which visa types qualify, whether there are any gaps in your leave, how many days you have spent outside the UK across rolling 12-month windows, whether you hold the correct English language evidence, whether you have passed the Life in the UK Test, and whether you have held your current visa for at least 12 months at the time of application.</p>

<p>Misunderstandings around visa gaps, overstay periods, and absence calculations are among the most common causes of unexpected problems at the ILR application stage.</p>
